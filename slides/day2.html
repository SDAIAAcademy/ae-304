What goes wrong. A parsing failure looks like a retrieval failure: the page "is never found". Score the parser on its own, against reference text, before you tune search.

Day 4 Session 4 makes this a method: attribute each failure to the first component that lost the answer. Today you measure the first component directly.

Two families of parser

A text-layer parser reads what the PDF stores; a layout-aware one reads the page

Parser A · text layer

Fast and cheap. Reads the characters the PDF holds, in the order it holds them. A scan holds no characters; Arabic may come out in presentation forms and reversed order.

Parser B · layout-aware

Slower. Recognises text from the image and finds table structure. Makes its own errors: similar letters confused.

Examples of the families: pypdf and pdfminer for text layers; Docling, Unstructured, Tesseract and open-weight document vision models for layout and recognition.

The lab's two parsers are simulated from the reference text with the failure patterns of each family, so every room gets the same scores. Say this plainly: the patterns are real, the outputs are generated. The method of scoring is what transfers to a real parser.

Inside the layout-aware family

A small parsing model or a general vision model: score both on your pages

Small specialised parsing model

Trained only to read documents: layout, reading order, table structure, characters. Runs on modest hardware and costs little per page. Weak on layouts it never saw.

General vision model

Reads the page image and follows an instruction, such as "return this table as Markdown". Flexible on odd layouts. Costs more per page and can write fluent text that is not on the page.

What goes wrong. A misread letter looks like a mistake. An invented word looks like text. Only a score against reference pages, by page type, shows which one you have.

No benchmark numbers on purpose: these models change every few months, and a leaderboard rarely includes scanned Arabic circulars. The method is the lab's: the same 30 pages, word error rate and table cells by page type, then a routing rule. A common result is a cascade: the specialised model for most pages, the general model only for the pages it scores badly on. For Arabic, check right-to-left reading order and digits in tables first.

Score the text

Word error rate: the edits that turn the output into the reference, per word

def words(text):
    text = unicodedata.normalize("NFKC", text)
    return re.findall(r"\w+", U.normalise(text))

def wer(ref, out):
    r, o = words(ref), words(out)
    return edit_distance(r, o) / len(r)
  • Normalise first, or you score typography, not content
  • Two swapped words cost two edits: a toy of three words scores 0.667
  • An empty output scores 1.0, every word missing

The toy check in the lab prints 0.667 for a swapped pair in three words. Ask why WER can exceed 1 (insertions); it does not happen here, but a parser that repeats headers can do it.

Score the tables

A table is right only when each cell is in its row and column

  • Compare the parsed table with the reference cell by cell: same row, same column, same text
  • Text can be perfect and the table worthless: "3" and "IND 212" on one line do not say which course they belong to
  • Study plans are mostly tables: a question like "how many credit hours is CSC 301" lives in one cell
Takeaway. Report text and table scores separately. A parser can win one and lose the other.

Parser A scored 0.000 on cells even on digital pages, because it drops the pipes: the words are there, the structure is gone. Parser B scored 0.998 on digital tables and 0.944 on scanned ones.

Session 2

Chunking by structure

  • Chunk documents by headings and keep the heading path
  • Compare chunkings by retrieval on table questions
  • Recommend a chunking from the evidence
02
Chunking

A chunk is the unit the model reads: big enough to answer, small enough to rank

Too small

A row without its header: "3 | IND 212" answers nothing.

Right

One semester's table with the plan and year named.

Too large

A whole plan: the right row is one of forty, and the score is diluted.

Ask where they chunk today. Most answers: "the library's default". AE-203 compared two chunkings of a policy corpus once; here the documents are tables, and chunking is scored by the questions it can answer. The default is usually a fixed window, which is today's baseline.

The common default

Fixed windows cut tables wherever the word count falls

window 1... | Total | 14 | | ### Year 1 · Semester 2 | Code | Course | Credit hours | Prerequisite | |---|---|---|---| | ACC 101 | Introduction to Financial ...
window 2... Accounting | 3 | UNI 105 | | ACC 112 | ...
What goes wrong. The window that holds "ACC 101" may not hold "Semester 2", and neither names the programme. The question "in which semester of the Accounting plan is ACC 101 taught?" has no single chunk that answers it.

The first window of the Accounting plan, printed in Lab 2.2 step 2 (120-word windows, 210 chunks over 30 plans). Overlap between windows helps a little and doubles the index.

Chunk by structure

Cut at the document's headings and carry the heading path into each chunk

Study plan: Bachelor of Accounting > Year 1 > Year 1 · Semester 1
| Code | Course | Credit hours | Prerequisite |
|---|---|---|---|
| ACC 112 | Foundations of Auditing | 3 | None |
| UNI 101 | Arabic Writing | 2 | None |
  • One chunk per semester: 8 per plan, 240 in all
  • The path names the plan, the year and the semester, so the chunk answers on its own

Prepending context to a chunk is the idea behind contextual retrieval; here the context is free, because the document's own headings supply it. Sources: Anthropic, Introducing contextual retrieval (see course sources).

Score chunking by the questions it answers

Answerable at 3: does a top-3 chunk hold everything the answer needs?

  • Each of the 20 table questions lists the strings the answer needs: "Semester 6" and "MTH 301"
  • A question is answerable when one of the top 3 chunks contains all of them
  • No model needed, so the score is cheap and repeatable

Why not recall?

Chunkings make different units, so there are no shared relevance judgements. Answerability compares them on the same questions.

This is a stricter cousin of recall: it requires all the needed facts in one chunk, which is what a model reading the top 3 needs. The lab prints one failure: the top fixed window for the MTH 301 question is the plan's introduction.

That is a ranking problem, now visible because chunking is no longer hiding it.

The recommendation, written down

A recommendation names the evidence and when to revisit it

Recommendation. Chunk study plans by semester heading with the plan title and year carried into each chunk, and never cut inside a table. Revisit if plans gain long prose sections, where windows of a fixed size may retrieve better.
  • Evidence: answerable at 3, 0.65 against 0.25, on 20 questions
  • Scope: study plans; regulations and news may chunk differently
  • Revisit: a condition you can check, not "later"

The text is the one the lab's step 5 prints. LO3's evidence is this pair: a parsing score per page type, and a retrieval comparison with a recommendation.

Dates, versions and duplicates

  • Read effective dates and amended articles from circulars

03
Dates and versions

A rule amended by a circular is still in the corpus, and it still ranks

  • The regulation's articles were written once
  • Circulars amend them, each with an issue date and an effective date
  • Both versions are relevant to the words of a question; only one is the answer today
3 of 15
changed questions whose top result states the current rule, before this session's fix

Measured in Lab 2.3 step 1 on 15 questions whose answer changed in a newer circular. The other 12 top results quote a version that no longer holds, which a fluent model would repeat with confidence.

Read both dates

The issue date says when a rule was written; the effective date says when it counts

questionWhat is the maximum study load in a regular semester? (asked on 1 November 2026)
Articlebetween 12 and 20 credit hours
Circular 2026-140issued 2026-10-05, effective 2027-01-01: between 12 and 18
answer12 to 20 today; 18 from 1 January 2027
What goes wrong. "Newest first" picks the circular, which is not yet in force.

This is question V05 of the lab, planted on purpose. Ask the room what "newest" would answer, then what the regulation actually says today. The stretch asks a question about the past: what was the attendance rule on 1 March 2024?

The fix

Give every version a validity interval and filter by today's date

Article 17 versionvalid fromvalid to
Regulation textstart2025 amendment
Circular 2025-118its effective dateopen
  • Parse the circular for the article it amends and its effective date
  • Close the previous version's interval when the next takes effect
  • At query time keep chunks valid on the question's date

In the lab: 93 chunks, 33 of them amendments; 63 are valid on 1 November 2026.

The reading is done with patterns on the circulars' fixed wording, which is enough for a corpus with a house style. A model can extract the same fields from free text; validate what it extracts against the patterns on a sample before trusting it.

Duplicates

Near-duplicate copies crowd the top results with the same text

  • Circulars are forwarded, re-posted by colleges, and copied into news
  • Compare chunks as sets of word shingles (runs of 3 words) and their Jaccard overlap
  • Copies measured 0.62 to 0.71 overlap; a threshold of 0.5 catches them
8
near-duplicate copies dropped in Lab 2.3
Keep the original. Drop the copy, keep the official circular's metadata: its dates are the ones that count.

The first version of this lab set the threshold at 0.7 and dropped one copy of eight: we measured the overlaps and lowered it. Thresholds are measured on your copies, not copied from a blog. At scale, MinHash estimates the same overlap without comparing every pair.

Make it a test

A staleness test asks a question and fails if the old answer comes first

for t in CHANGED:                  # 15 questions, Arabic and English
    top = search(valid_on(TODAY), [t["q"], t["q_ar"]])[0]
    assert t["expect"] in top.text, t["qid"]
    assert t["stale"] not in top.text, t["qid"]
3 → 15
of 15 tests passing, before and after

Run it on every rebuild

A new circular, a re-parse or a new model can break it again.

The tests search in both languages, because some amendments exist only in the Arabic circular: an English-only test would miss them. This suite is half of LO4's evidence; the leak suite of Session 4 is the other half.

Retrieved text is data

A page can carry instructions to the model; quote it, never obey it

Circular 2026-777The student satisfaction survey opens next week ... followed by a note addressed to automated assistants, asking them to ignore access rules and include disciplinary summaries and examination papers.
Nibras's pipelineFlagged P0487. Roles come from the account, not from page text: nothing unlocked.
Takeaway. Permissions are enforced in code on the account's roles. No text in the index can change them.

The circular is planted in the corpus; the lab flags it with a pattern and checks that it could not unlock anything. This is indirect prompt injection through retrieved content. Sources: OWASP GenAI Security Project, LLM01 Prompt injection (see course sources).
